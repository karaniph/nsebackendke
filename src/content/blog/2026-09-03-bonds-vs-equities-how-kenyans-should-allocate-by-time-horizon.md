## Why this matters
For a Kenyan retail investor, the split between bonds and equities is the single most practical lever to control risk and growth over time. A sensible mix can dampen volatility while preserving upside, helping you hit longer-term goals like education or retirement. Get the allocation wrong, and you either chase yield you can’t sustain or endure unnecessary swings that tempt premature selling.

## The concept
Bonds are essentially loans to governments or companies, paying fixed interest over time. Equities are ownership stakes in a company, offering dividends and price appreciation but with higher volatility. Allocation is the decision about how much of your portfolio sits in each bucket. The core tradeoffs are clear: bonds tend to be steadier and predictably income-generating; equities offer higher potential returns but bring more risk and drawdowns. Your horizon and appetite for risk determine the tilt between the two.

Equities trade on the NSE with liquid counters such as **SCOM** (Safaricom), **KCB** (Kenya Commercial Bank), and **EABL** (East Africa Breweries), which provide growth potential and dividend streams. A diversified local allocation typically mixes safe, income-generating bonds with growth-oriented equities to capture upside while preserving capital during drawdowns. Rebalancing is easier when you understand how market signals—like interest-rate expectations and earnings cycles—affect both sides of the portfolio.

## Worked example
- Step 1: Clarify your horizon. If you expect to need the money in the foreseeable future, favor a ballast tilt with more bonds. If your goal is long-term growth and you can tolerate volatility, tilt toward equities.
- Step 2: State your risk tolerance. A cautious investor prioritizes stability and predictable income; a growth-focused investor accepts more swings in exchange for higher potential returns.
- Step 3: Draft a baseline allocation in spirit. Think in broad sleeves: a bond sleeve for ballast and an equity sleeve for growth, plus a rebalancing trigger when risk signals change.
- Step 4: Build the sleeves using Kenya-facing options. For the bond sleeve, consider high-quality Kenyan government bonds or stable corporate issues available in the market. For the equity sleeve, include familiar local counters such as **SCOM**, **KCB**, and **EABL** to capture dividend income and capital appreciation.
- Step 5: Apply discipline. Rebalance periodically—at a minimum when your personal risk tolerance or time horizon changes, or when major macro signals shift. If interest-rate expectations rise and bond prices feel sensitive, you can adjust the bond sleeve toward shorter-duration issues; if growth is leading and volatility is tolerable, reallocate toward equities.
- Step 6: Use a simple guardrail approach. Keep the core idea that bonds provide ballast and equities drive growth, with the relative emphasis adjusted as your situation evolves. The goal is a predictable glide path toward your objectives, not chasing performance every year.

Illustrative takeaway: a long-horizon investor leans more on equities to participate in compounding and growth, while maintaining a bond sleeve to smooth returns. A short-horizon investor leans more on bonds for capital preservation and near-term liquidity, using a smaller equity sleeve to capture any upside when conditions cooperate. The exact mix will depend on your personal situation, but the logic remains constant: risk, yield, and time horizon determine the tilt, not the other way around.

## Common mistakes
- Overallocating to equities just because markets have recently risen. Momentum can reverse; ensure the plan fits your horizon and risk comfort.
- Ignoring duration risk in bonds. Long-duration issues can swing more with rate moves; shorter duration can reduce sensitivity in a rising-rate environment.
- Failing to rebalance after a shift in personal circumstances or market regimes. Without discipline, small changes in markets can drift you away from your target risk level.
- Treating domestic and global assets as interchangeable. Local NSE dynamics—dividends, inflation, and Kenya-specific tax treatment—matter for your real returns.
- Forgetting liquidity needs. If you might need cash soon, too-heavy equity exposure can force distress selling at unfavorable moments.

## Your checklist
- Define your time horizon in practical terms (short, medium, long) and set a corresponding risk posture.
- List bond and equity options available in the Kenyan market you’re comfortable with (government, corporate bonds; **SCOM**, **KCB**, **EABL** equities).
- Decide on a baseline tilt that matches your horizon and risk tolerance, with explicit rebalancing triggers.
- Monitor macro signals (rates, inflation, corporate earnings) and adjust the sleeves accordingly.
- Revisit the plan at least annually or when life events require it.

## FAQ
- How should a beginner start with bonds vs equities in Kenya?
  Start with a conservative bond sleeve for stability and a small equity sleeve for growth, then progressively tilt as you gain comfort and clarity on your horizon.
- Can I switch to all bonds if I’m worried about volatility?
  Yes, you can reduce the equity sleeve to preserve capital, but consider keeping some growth exposure to avoid missing out on long-run opportunities.
- Should I include international assets or stick to Kenyan markets?
  For many retail investors, a core domestic allocation plus selective international exposure can help diversify, but start with familiarity and tax efficiency first.
