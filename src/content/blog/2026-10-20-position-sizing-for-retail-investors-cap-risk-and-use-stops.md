## Why this matters
Capital discipline is the thin line between compounding gains and blowing up a small account. For retail investors on the NSE, one oversized bet in a thin session can erase weeks of work. Position sizing gives you a guardrail: it caps how much you risk on any single idea and keeps you in the market long enough to see what actually works.

In practice, you want to protect capital first so you can stay invested as the NASI moves and as names like BRIT, TPSE and CTUM make intraday moves. If flows stay muted, it becomes even more important to keep your bets small and your stops tight. The payoff is straightforward: fewer blown accounts, more consistent participation, better odds of catching the bigger moves over weeks or months.

## The concept
Position sizing is the process of deciding how many shares or how much money to put into a trade based on how much risk you’re willing to take. Key terms you should know first:
- Risk per trade: how much you’re willing to lose if the trade goes against you, usually expressed as a monetary amount or a percentage of your portfolio.
- Stop loss: a preplanned exit price that limits how far you let a trade run against you.
- Position size: the number of shares or the dollar value you actually buy based on your risk limit and stop distance.
- Risk tolerance: the amount of money you’re comfortable losing in a single trade or over a week or month.

The core idea is simple: your maximum loss on any trade equals your risk per trade, not your entire portfolio. If you set risk per trade as a fixed percentage of your capital, you automatically scale your bets as your account grows or shrinks. The discipline comes from placing a stop where your defined loss will be triggered, and from sizing the position so that the stop loss distance times the number of shares equals your risk cap.

## How it works on the NSE
On the Nairobi Securities Exchange you should prioritize liquidity and price discovery. Thin sessions, as noted in today’s context, reduce cushion and increase the risk of gaps. That’s why you should size you positions with liquidity in mind and keep stops within reasonable market realities.
- Start with a risk cap per trade. A common starter rule for a retail account is 1% to 2% of your total capital per trade. For a 1,000,000 KES portfolio, that means a 10,000 to 20,000 KES risk limit per idea.
- Determine stop distance using real price action, not a rough guess. If you buy a stock at 25 KES and your stop is at 23 KES, the per-share risk is 2 KES. The more volatile the stock, the larger the stop distance you may require; the more liquidity a stock has, the smaller the chance of a stop being violated by a quick move.
- Compute shares or dollar value. With a 10,000 KES risk cap and a 2 KES stop distance, you can buy up to 5,000 shares. At 25 KES per share, that position would represent a 125,000 KES exposure if executed.
- Consider diversification. Even with a disciplined risk cap, a single large position can dominate your risk. Use a practical cap on total exposure to any one stock and spread bets across a few liquid names like SCOM, KCB, or EQTY.
- Include costs and taxes. Trading costs and potential stamp duty or fees shave your actual risk and your upside. Include these in your stop level or in your risk assumption.
- Use market context. When debt-market signals or thin volume color the tape, tightening risk controls makes sense. If flows stay muted, you may prefer fewer, higher-probability setups with smaller position sizes.

In short, position sizing on the NSE is a two-step discipline: decide how much you’re willing to lose on a trade, then place a stop so that loss never exceeds that limit. This keeps you in the game long enough to learn what actually works in Kenyan equities, particularly in a market where liquidity can swing intraday.

## Worked example
Let’s walk through a concrete, repeatable calculation you can apply today.
- Portfolio size: 1,000,000 KES
- Risk per trade: 1% of portfolio = 10,000 KES
- Entry price: 25 KES
- Stop distance: 2 KES (stop at 23 KES)
- Maximum shares you can hold based on risk: 10,000 / 2 = 5,000 shares
- Position value if you buy at 25 KES: 5,000 shares × 25 KES = 125,000 KES

With these inputs, your worst-case loss if the stock hits your stop is 10,000 KES, which is exactly 1% of your portfolio. If the stock moves in your favor, you can consider revising the stop to breakeven or trailing it to lock in gains, but only after the trade proves its risk/reward setup. If you want to be more conservative, reduce risk per trade to 0.5% (5,000 KES) and either cut the number of shares to 2,000 or pick a higher-stop distance so the math still supports a reasonable reward.

A few practical tweaks you’ll encounter in real markets:
- If you’re trading multiple ideas, you should not let one position dominate your overall risk. A simple rule is to cap total exposure to any single stock at a portion of your total risk budget, then allocate the rest across other names.
- Always account for liquidity. Even with a good plan, a stock that trades lightly can gap beyond your stop. If a gap is plausible based on recent price history, you should reduce the position size or widen the stop so the risk remains within your cap.
- Use price alerts and plan exits in advance. If you’re not watching the tape constantly, a pre-set alert around your stop level helps you avoid letting a losing trade drift into a bigger loss due to hesitation.

Worked example takeaway: position sizing ties the amount you risk directly to your portfolio size and the distance to your stop. It’s not about predicting the next price move; it’s about controlling the damage if you’re wrong and preserving capital for the next opportunity.

## Common mistakes
- Overloading a single name because of a hot idea or a fast move without recalibrating risk per trade.
- Ignoring costs and taxes when calculating risk; the net loss can exceed your stop distance if you don’t account for fees.
- Using a fixed dollar amount of shares without adjusting for price changes or volatility; a 5,000-share plan on a 5 KES stock compares differently to a 25 KES stock.
- Not setting or adhering to stops; letting winners turn into losers is a common trap when emotions take over.
- Failing to diversify; piling into one sector or one small-cap counter exposes you to idiosyncratic risk.
- Forgetting to update risk as your account grows or shrinks; a 1% rule needs to scale with capital to stay meaningful.

## Your checklist
- Define risk per trade as a percent of your total capital.
- Determine stop distance using recent price action and liquidity.
- Compute maximum shares or dollar value with the risk cap and stop distance.
- Verify the total exposure fits your diversification rules.
- Include trading costs in the risk calculation.
- Set price alerts and have a clear exit plan before you enter a trade.
- Reassess risk after any new position is opened and adjust if needed.

## FAQ
- How do I decide the right risk per trade?
  Start with 1% for a smaller account and 1%–2% for a larger account. You can tighten or loosen based on your experience, volatility of the name, and how well you stick to the plan.
- What happens if the stock gaps through my stop?
  Gaps can blow past your stop; that’s why you must consider wider stops in highly volatile or illiquid names or choose smaller position sizes when risk of gaps is high.
- Should I keep the same stop across trades?
  Not necessarily. Stops should reflect each name’s volatility, liquidity, and your assessment of what constitutes an acceptable loss on that idea.
