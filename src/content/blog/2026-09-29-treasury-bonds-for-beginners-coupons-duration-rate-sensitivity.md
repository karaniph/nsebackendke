## The concept
- Coupon: a bond’s periodic interest payment, calculated as a percentage of its face value (par value). Coupons are typically fixed for the life of the bond and paid at regular intervals.
- Face value (par value): the amount repaid at maturity. Your coupon payments are a percentage of this amount.
- Yield: the rate that investors expect to earn if they hold the bond to maturity, reflecting both coupon income and price changes.
- Duration: a measure of a bond’s sensitivity to interest-rate moves. In plain terms, longer duration means bigger price moves when rates change, and shorter duration means smaller moves.
- Rate sensitivity: how much a bond’s price will change when market yields shift. This is driven by the bond’s coupons and how far away its maturity is.
On the NSE, Treasury bonds fall under the Fixed Income & Money Market segment and are priced with reference to prevailing market yields. The current environment—policy rate around 8.75% and short yields in the vicinity of 8.778%—adds context for how new issues and existing bonds will behave as rates drift.

## How it works on the NSE
- The instrument: Government Treasury bonds are issued by the Kenyan Treasury and traded on the NSE via the Fixed Income & Money Market platform. Prices reflect the going market yield for that tenor, plus the bond’s coupon schedule.
- Coupon payments: these are built into the bond’s cash flow; you receive regular coupon cash as defined by the bond’s terms.
- Price and yield link: when yields rise, prices typically fall to align with the higher discount rate; when yields fall, prices rise. This is the core of duration-related risk and opportunity.
- Benchmark context: the policy rate (currently around 8.75% on the latest data) and nearby short-term yields (e.g., 91D at about 8.778%) provide a frame for what kind of rate moves might matter for a given tenor.
- Reinvestment: coupon payments can be reinvested; the rate you can reinvest at affects your total return, especially for longer-horizon holders.

## Worked example
- Step 1: Identify the bond’s cash flows. A Treasury bond promises a stream of fixed coupon payments at regular intervals and the repayment of the face value at maturity.
- Step 2: Determine the discount rate. Use the prevailing market yield for a bond of similar tenor as the discount rate. In practice, this means looking at current yields around the area where the bond sits on the curve (the NSE and CBK window provide these quotes).
- Step 3: Calculate price from cash flows. The price is the present value of all future coupon payments plus the present value of the face value repaid at maturity. In math terms, P = sum of each coupon discounted at the yield, plus the par value discounted at the same yield.
- Step 4: Bring in duration. If yields move by a small amount, the approximate price change is ΔP ≈ -D × P × Δy, where D is duration and Δy is the change in yield (in percentage points). This is a rule of thumb to gauge sensitivity without heavy math.
- Step 5: Consider convexity for larger moves. Real-world moves aren’t perfectly linear, so an extra convexity term helps refine estimates when rates move more than a whisper.
- Step 6: Interpret for a practical decision. If you’re buying and you expect yields to drift lower, a bond with a longer duration could rise more in price, but you’re also locking in a longer wait for coupon income. If you expect rates to rise, shorter-duration bonds will dampen price declines.
- Why this matters today: with the current backdrop of the 8.75% policy rate and the 8.778% short-end yield, a beginner can focus on locating bonds with a tenor and coupon profile that matches their income needs and risk tolerance. The NSE’s bond quotes reflect those dynamics in real time, and a disciplined investor uses duration as a compass rather than chasing headline yields alone.

## Common mistakes
- Confusing coupon rate with yield: a high coupon doesn’t guarantee a high return if the price has dropped; yield accounts for price-paid and income via coupons.
- Ignoring duration when choosing tenor: longer maturities aren’t free money; they expose you to bigger price swings as rates move.
- Overlooking reinvestment risk: coupon income must be reinvested to compound your return; a rising-rate environment helps reinvestment, a falling one hurts.
- Forgetting the payment schedule: missed coupon dates or irregular payment calendars can surprise a new buyer; verify the schedule before buying.
- Focusing only on current yield: a bond with a high yield now may have poor price prospects if its tenor is long and rates rise; consider total return, not just yield.

## Your checklist
- Identify the bond’s coupon schedule and face value.
- Check the tenor and approximate duration relative to your horizon.
- Compare the bond’s yield to nearby benchmarks for similar tenor.
- Confirm the coupon type (fixed) and payment frequency (semi-annual is common, but verify).
- Note the redemption at maturity and any special features or call provisions.
- Review current policy rate and nearby short yields to gauge rate-move context on the NSE.

## FAQ
- Q: How do coupons affect my return beyond the yield? A: Coupons give you periodic income, which you can reinvest; the combination of coupon cash flows and any price changes due to rate moves determines total return.
- Q: Why does longer duration mean bigger price swings? A: Because more of the bond’s value comes from cash flows farther in the future, and those cash flows are discounted more when yields rise, making the price fall more.
- Q: How do I actually buy Treasury bonds on the NSE? A: Use the NSE’s Fixed Income & Money Market platform via your broker, and settle trades through the CBK window. Primary issuance is by the government; secondary trading happens on the exchange.
