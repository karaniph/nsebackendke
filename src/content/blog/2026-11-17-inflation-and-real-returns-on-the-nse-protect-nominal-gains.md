## Why this matters
Inflation eats into buying power, so a stock that looks up 6% on your screen might not actually grow your purchasing power. For Kenyan retail investors, understanding real returns helps you judge whether your NSE gains are genuinely growing wealth or just keeping pace with rising prices.

In practice, this matters for how you compare stock moves, dividends and overall portfolio performance in a world where price levels shift every year. The difference between nominal gains and real gains can explain why some portfolios drift from your financial goals even when they look like they’re doing fine on the surface.

## The concept
Nominal return is the percentage change in an investment’s price plus any dividends paid, without adjusting for inflation. Real return strips out inflation to show how much your purchasing power has actually grown. Inflation is the rate at which you need more money to buy the same basket of goods and services. A handy way to think about real return is that it tells you how much you actually gained after price levels rise.

For most investors, a rough rule of thumb is real return roughly equals nominal return minus inflation. A more exact approach uses the formula real return = (1 + nominal) / (1 + inflation) − 1. If inflation climbs above your nominal gains, your real return turns negative.

## How it works on the NSE
The Nairobi Securities Exchange trades in Kenyan shillings, with equity prices moving alongside earnings, dividends, and macro signals from the central bank. In a thin session, price moves like LKL up 6.61% or SGL up 3.70% can look big, but inflation still determines whether those moves translate into real wealth. When the market is quiet and liquidity light, inflation expectations and policy rate signals have outsized effects on real returns because they influence discount rates and future cash flows.

At the index level, broad inflation pressure matters more for growth-oriented stocks and for names with price-to-earnings multiples that are sensitive to discount-rate changes. And while fixed income yields respond directly to inflation expectations, equities rely on earnings growth and dividend policy to beat inflation over time. The takeaway: nominal gains in a single session are not a guarantee of real gains unless earnings, dividends and price appreciation outpace inflation over your horizon.

In the current local context, sector performance is varied: banking and financials may show mixed price moves; consumer and industrials can offer different inflation hedges via pricing power and dividend dynamics. ## Worked example
Step 1: Identify a nominal gain to anchor the example. Suppose you held a stock that rose 6.61% over your chosen horizon (LKL in the latest session showed a 6.61% intraday move). This is your nominal return Rnom.

Step 2: Estimate the inflation rate over the same horizon. Inflation is the rate at which prices rise; in practice you’ll have a forecast or a range to work with. For illustration, think of i as your expected inflation rate for the period you’re measuring.

Step 3: Compute the real return. Use the simple approximation real return ≈ nominal return − inflation for quick checks, or the exact formula real return = (1 + Rnom) / (1 + i) − 1 for a precise result. If Rnom is 6.61% and inflation i is 5% (illustrative), the rough real return is about 1.61%. Using the exact formula yields a similar outcome for small rates.

Step 4: Interpret the result. If i is lower than the nominal gain, you’ve achieved positive real growth; if i is higher, your real wealth actually contracted. The exact number matters, but the direction is the key signal for your portfolio plan.

Step 5: Apply to a diversified mix. A portfolio with several names, some paying dividends and others delivering price gains, has a better chance of real growth than a lone pick especially in a high-inflation environment. Consider how much of your return comes from price moves versus income, and how inflation could alter the real outcome.

Worked example takeaway: real returns depend on both price changes and inflation. A 6.61% nominal gain can look strong, but if inflation is running higher, your real return may be weak or negative. Conversely, modest nominal gains in a low-inflation environment can outperform big gains in high inflation.

## Common mistakes
- Confusing nominal and real returns without checking inflation. Always compare both to judge true wealth growth.
- Ignoring the impact of dividends and their real value. Dividends matter especially when price moves are limited.
- Relying on a single stock’s move as a portfolio signal. A diversified mix with earnings growth and price appreciation is more likely to deliver real gains.
- Using short windows to judge real returns. Inflation is a longer-run factor; align horizons accordingly.
- Overlooking currency and macro shifts. Exchange-rate movements and domestic price levels can alter real outcomes for investors with foreign exposures or costs in local currency terms.

## Your checklist
- Identify the nominal return of each equity you hold over your horizon.
- Estimate inflation for that same horizon or use a credible forecast range.
- Compute real return using real return = (1 + nominal) / (1 + inflation) − 1 or the rough nominal minus inflation rule of thumb.
- Compare across assets to see which holdings offer positive real returns under your inflation scenario.
- Consider dividend growth, earnings resilience and pricing power as you reweight for real performance.
- Maintain diversification to spread inflation risk across sectors and cash flows.

## FAQ
- What is real return in plain terms?
  Real return is how much your investment grows after removing the effect of rising prices; it shows true wealth growth.
- How should I estimate inflation for my portfolio?
  Use a credible forecast for your horizon and scenario-test a range. Inflation expectations are part of your planning assumptions.
- Should I chase big dividend yields to beat inflation? 
  Dividend yields help, but you also need price appreciation and earnings growth to sustain real gains over time; don’t rely on dividends alone.
