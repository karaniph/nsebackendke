## Why this matters
Chasing the highest headline yield on the NSE can burn you when payments aren’t sustainable. For Kenyan retail investors, a payout that looks rich today can vanish next year if it’s funded by reserves or one‑offs rather than steady earnings and cash flow. The point is simple: yield is not a stand‑alone signal—the durability of the payout matters more than the headline figure.

Such actions can move prices and cloud the true yield unless you check sustainability. ## The concept
- Dividend yield is the annual dividend per share divided by the price per share. It’s a way to compare how much cash you get back for each unit of price today.
- Payout quality matters: a big yield is only as good as its sustainability. Key signals include the payout ratio (dividends relative to earnings per share) and cash‑flow coverage (can the business fund the payout from operating cash flow without drawing down reserves).
- A sustainable payout typically shows a stable or growing dividend history, supported by earnings and free cash flow, not by one‑off gains or balance‑sheet borrowing.

In plain terms: a high yield is nice, but a higher likelihood of a dividend cut or a washout of cash flow is a bigger risk you should price in.

## How it works on the NSE
- On the Nairobi Securities Exchange, dividend announcements and corporate actions (cash dividends, scrip dividends, or bonuses) are common around earnings seasons. This creates visible yield opportunities, but also the potential for sudden changes if a payout is curtailed or a bonus affects the stock price.
- The headline yield can look attractive after a price drop prompted by the same dividend news. Yet if the payout is not backed by earnings or free cash flow, the higher yield may be illusory.
- Ex‑dividend dates matter: the price typically adjusts when the stock trades without the right to the upcoming dividend, which can temporarily boost or depress yield measurements depending on what follows in cash flow.
- The source data from NSEinsider’s current feed notes several issuers paying dividends or issuing bonuses. While these signals are useful, they should be interpreted in the context of each company’s earnings trajectory and cash‑flow generation.

In practice, you should compare the yield offered by a stock with its payout sustainability metrics and with peers in the same sector. A company with a modest headline yield but solid cash flow and a history of growing or stable dividends may be a better anchor than a one‑off high yield from a thinly supported payout.

## Worked example
- Step 1: Gather your inputs. You need the price per share today (P) and the annual dividend per share (D). Then pull the earnings per share (EPS) and, if possible, free cash flow per share (FCF).
- Step 2: Compute the raw yield. Yield = D / P. This tells you what the market currently pays for each shilling of price in dividend cash, before considering sustainability.
- Step 3: Check payout quality. Compute the payout ratio = D / EPS. If the payout ratio is well above your confidence threshold (and/or above the sector norm), this flags risk: the payout may not be fully supported by earnings.
- Step 4: Check cash‑flow coverage. Compute cash‑flow coverage = FCF / D. If cash flow doesn’t cover the dividend by a comfortable margin, the payout is relying on non‑recurring items or borrowings, which is a red flag.
- Step 5: Look at dividend history. A stable or gently rising dividend over several years earns more credibility than a single year of high payouts tied to an earnings spike or a one‑time gain.
- Step 6: Compare with peers. If a high‑yield counter sits among peers with stronger cash flow generation and lower payout risk, you may prefer the latter even if the headline yield is lower.

Note: Use actual prices, dividends, earnings, and cash flow from company disclosures when you run the numbers. This framework is the how‑to; your inputs determine whether a yield looks safe or suspect.

Illustrative approach (no real figures shown): If D and P are plugged in and the resulting Yield looks unusually high, scan for a payout ratio that exceeds earnings capability or a cash‑flow gap that suggests reserves or debt supporting the payout. If you find a comfortable cushion—P/E‑adjusted value, stable earnings, and adequate FCF coverage—the yield signal is more credible; if not, the high yield is a trap.

## Common mistakes
- Chasing the highest headline yield without checking sustainability.
- Ignoring the ex‑dividend date and price movement around payout events.
- Taking payout ratio at face value without verifying earnings quality or free cash flow.
- Missing one‑off or special dividends that inflate the stated yield temporarily.
- Comparing yields without adjusting for sector norms or risk factors.
- Relying on a single data point; you need a history of dividends and cash flow to judge durability.

## Your checklist
- Gather: price (P), annual dividend per share (D), earnings per share (EPS), and free cash flow per share (FCF).
- Compute: Yield = D / P and Payout ratio = D / EPS; Cash‑flow coverage = FCF / D.
- Assess trend: review historical dividends and cash flow trends over at least 3–5 years.
- Context: compare with sector peers and consider the impact of ex‑dividend dates and any planned corporate actions (bonuses, stock splits).
- Decide: prefer a sustainable payout with solid cash flow coverage over a flashy but fragile high yield.

## FAQ
- What is a payout ratio and why does it matter?
  The payout ratio is the portion of earnings paid as dividends. A very high ratio suggests earnings are being stretched to fund the dividend and could signal risk if earnings deteriorate.
- When is yield misleading?
  Yield looks large when prices fall or when dividends include one‑offs. In both cases, sustainability may be questionable if earnings or cash flow don’t back it up.
- Should I ignore a high yield if payout quality seems solid?
  Not at all. A high yield with robust cash flow and modest growth in dividends can be attractive, but you should still watch for changing fundamentals and ensure there’s a clear path to continued payments.
