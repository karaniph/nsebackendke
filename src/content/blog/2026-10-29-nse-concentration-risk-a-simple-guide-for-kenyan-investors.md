## Why this matters
Concentration risk can erase months of gains when a few holdings swing together. On the NSE, a large portion of capital in a single sector or a handful of names can magnify losses if that area falters. Diversification isn’t a luxury; it’s a practical guardrail that helps a retail investor stay in the game when markets get choppy.

A disciplined approach to risk means thinking in terms of sectors, correlations, and how your entire slice of the market behaves, not just chasing last week’s winners. The payoff is smoother performance and more capital available to deploy when opportunities arise.

## The concept
Concentration risk is the danger that a disproportionate share of your money sits in a few names or a single sector. Sector balance means spreading exposure across different parts of the economy so a single downturn doesn’t drag the whole portfolio. Correlation is the degree to which two assets move in tandem; low correlation helps diversify risk rather than amplifying it.

Diversification is not random; it’s a deliberate structure. You’re aiming for multiple uncorrelated drivers of return, so a bad day in one area doesn’t derail the whole plan. The goal is a portfolio that reacts more to broad market signals than to a single stock pulse.

## How it works on the NSE
The Nairobi Securities Exchange hosts a mix of banks, telecoms, consumer staples, and other sectors. Concentration risk on the NSE often shows up when a large chunk of capital sits in a few heavyweight counters or a single sector that dominates the index. Since most Kenyan stocks move in similar liquidity conditions, correlations tend to rise during market stress, which diminishes diversification benefits.

To manage this, investors should think in terms of sector exposure and cross-asset correlation, not just single-stock picks. Use a simple rule of thumb: avoid letting any one sector or pair of assets dominate your portfolio, and prefer additions that bring a different price movement pattern even if they promise steady dividends. This approach keeps you aligned with long-run risk tolerance rather than short-term hype.

In practice, price data from quick price lists can be sparse or not reveal key numbers. The real signal comes from how weights across holdings respond to risk and how correlated new additions are with the rest of the portfolio. Focus on structure, liquidity, and how diversification affects potential volatility.

## Worked example
- Current portfolio consists of three buckets A, B, and C with weights WA, WB, WC that sum to one. The largest bucket represents the concentration risk and is the first target for rebalancing.
- Step 1: Identify the concentration. If WA is the dominant weight, you plan a reallocation to bring the portfolio into a more balanced shape without violating liquidity or tax considerations.
- Step 2: Allocate to underrepresented sectors or assets D, E that have low correlation with A. The goal is to increase diversification without forcing a loss of upside in your existing holdings.
- Step 3: After transfers, adjust weights so the sum is one again. The new weights WA', WB', WC', WD' reflect a broader mix rather than a single driver.
- Step 4: Check correlation across holdings. Ensure that the new additions do not move in tandem with A during stress periods, which would still undermine diversification.
- Step 5: Review expected risk against your tolerance. Use simple qualitative checks like whether the broad market pull would hurt this mix and adjust if necessary.
- Step 6: Rebalance on a timetable tied to your plan (for example, quarterly or after material moves in the market). Do not wait for just a handful of events to trigger changes.

This worked example uses symbolic weights to show how you would reduce a single concentrated exposure and replace it with assets from other parts of the market that behave differently under stress. It also highlights the importance of keeping liquidity and costs in mind when rebalancing.

## Common mistakes
- Chasing last year’s winners while ignoring the new risk; winners can abandon you when the market shifts.
- Overlooking correlation; two assets may seem different but move in lockstep during a downturn.
- Doing one-off tweaks without a plan; you need a repeatable process and calendar for rebalancing.
- Ignoring costs and taxes; frequent trades can erode returns and negate diversification benefits.
- Failing to update risk metrics after major life changes or market shifts; risk tolerance can drift over time.

## Your checklist
- Define a clear diversification target across sectors and asset types.
- Track the concentration by watching the largest holdings and the sector weights.
- Verify that new additions have a lower correlation with the rest of the portfolio.
- Set a practical rebalancing cadence and stick to it.
- Account for liquidity and transaction costs before executing any moves.

## FAQ
- What is concentration risk and why does it matter in Kenya? It’s the risk that a few holdings drive most of your returns; it matters because a bad move in that area can hit hard when you can least afford it.
- How do I know when to rebalance? Look for a single holding or sector that becomes a dominant portion of your portfolio and consider adding assets with different exposure patterns.
- Should I use index funds or single stocks for diversification? A mix can work; index exposure helps with broad diversification, while selective stocks let you express views in areas you understand best.
