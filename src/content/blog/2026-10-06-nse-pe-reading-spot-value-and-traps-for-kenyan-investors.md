## Why this matters
P/E is one of the simplest yardsticks for value in stocks, but it can mislead if used in isolation. For Kenyan investors on the NSE, learning what P/E tells you about a stock’s price relative to earnings helps you separate value from hype. ## The concept
P/E stands for price-earnings ratio. It is the price per share divided by earnings per share over a period, typically the last 12 months (trailing twelve months, or TTM). EPS is net income divided by the number of shares outstanding. A higher P/E can mean investors expect faster earnings growth, while a lower P/E can indicate slower growth or higher risk. P/E is most informative when you compare it across similar companies or against historical norms, not in isolation.

Note the difference between trailing P/E and forward P/E. Trailing uses actual reported earnings; forward uses expected future earnings. On the NSE, you usually derive P/E by taking the latest closing price and the most recent TTM EPS from results, then calculating price divided by EPS. P/E can be distorted by one-off items, accounting changes, or share buybacks, so context matters.

## How it works on the NSE
On the NSE, P/E is not printed as a single line on every quote. You compute it yourself from two pieces of data:
- Price per share: use the latest close for the stock you are analyzing.
- Earnings per share: use the trailing twelve months EPS reported by the company, or the last full-year EPS if TTM is not available.

Because earnings quality varies and results can swing, P/E on the NSE should be compared across peers in the same sector and across your own time horizon. A high P/E in a fast-growing tech stock can be sensible, while the same P/E in a mature utility may signal overvaluation if earnings aren’t keeping pace.

Limitations to keep in mind: if EPS is negative, P/E is not defined; if earnings are volatile, the P/E can bounce around; and one-off charges can distort the number. Also, macro conditions (like a tight money market or high inflation) can compress or inflate P/E interpretations even when fundamentals look steady.

## Worked example
Think of an illustrative stock called ABC Ltd. Suppose ABC trades at 200 KES and the trailing twelve months EPS is 8 KES. P/E = 200 / 8 = 25x.
- If price rises to 260 while EPS stays at 8, P/E becomes 32x, signaling the stock now looks more expensive relative to the same earnings base.
- If ABC grows EPS to 12 while price is 260, P/E = 260 / 12 ≈ 21.7x, meaning earnings growth is keeping the price from getting more expensive on a per-kilowatt basis of earnings.
- If price stays at 260 but EPS slips to 6, P/E ≈ 43.3x, indicating a large valuation jump relative to weaker earnings.

This illustrates two key ideas: P/E changes when price moves or when earnings move, and you must consider both sides to judge whether the stock is fairly priced for its growth and risk.

## Common mistakes
- Comparing P/E across sectors that have different growth profiles.
- Ignoring whether the P/E is trailing or forward, and using them interchangeably.
- Relying on P/E when EPS is irregular due to one-off items or Accounting changes.
- Ignoring earnings quality and whether earnings are sustainable or driven by non-operational items.
- Judging value solely by P/E without considering growth prospects, leverage, or cash flow.
- Using a single P/E figure without looking at sector or historical norms.
- Treating a high P/E as a buy signal without verifying upcoming catalysts or risks.

## Your checklist
- Confirm the EPS used is trailing twelve months or specify forward estimates if you’re using forward P/E.
- Use the latest closing price for the P/E calculation.
- Compare the stock’s P/E to its sector peers and to the stock’s own historical range.
- Check earnings quality and growth trajectory before drawing conclusions.
- Note any one-off items, restructuring costs, or extraordinary items that could skew EPS.
- Consider whether macro conditions (rates, liquidity) could justify a higher or lower P/E.

## FAQ
Q: Is a high P/E always a bad sign?
A: Not necessarily. It can reflect strong growth expectations, but you should verify that earnings are growing and sustainable rather than just priced in by investors.

Q: What if a company reports negative earnings?
A: P/E is not defined when EPS is negative. In that case, look at other metrics like price-to-sales or price-to-book, and assess why earnings are negative.

Q: Should I compare P/E across all stocks?
A: No. P/E makes the most sense among peers in the same sector with similar risk and growth profiles, and when you adjust for growth expectations.
