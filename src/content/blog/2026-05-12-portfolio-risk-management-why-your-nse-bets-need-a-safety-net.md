# Concept

Portfolio risk management isn’t about avoiding risk—it’s about *managing* it. Think of it like a seatbelt: you don’t drive slower because you’re wearing one, but you’re glad it’s there when the pothole hits. The core idea is **diversification**, measured by how much of your portfolio is exposed to any single asset, sector, or risk factor. The golden rule? No single holding should ever exceed 10% of your total portfolio value. Why? Because if that stock or sector tanks, your entire portfolio tanks with it. The math is brutal: if 20% of your portfolio is in Safaricom and it drops 15%, your portfolio just lost 3%—without even blinking. The formula is simple: **Portfolio Risk = (Weight of Asset A × Return of Asset A) + (Weight of Asset B × Return of Asset B) + ...** But here’s the catch: this only works if your assets don’t move in lockstep. If every stock on the NSE crashes when the shilling weakens, your diversification is a mirage.

Economic intuition? Markets price risk, and investors pay a premium for assets that *don’t* move in sync with the broader market. That’s why Kenyan investors flock to Safaricom and KCB—they’re liquid, they pay dividends, and they’re (mostly) stable. But stability is a double-edged sword. When the market turns, those same stocks can become anchors, dragging your portfolio down faster than a matatu swerving to avoid a pothole. The real magic of risk management isn’t just spreading your bets—it’s ensuring those bets aren’t all betting on the same horse.

This concept shines brightest in volatile markets. During a rate-hike cycle, defensive stocks (think banks and telecoms) might hold up better than cyclicals (like manufacturing or construction). But if you’re overloaded in one sector, even the sturdiest stocks can become liabilities. In a downturn, the least useful time to realize you’re overconcentrated is when your portfolio is already bleeding red.

# NSE Context

On the Nairobi Securities Exchange, risk management takes on a uniquely Kenyan flavor. The market is **highly concentrated**, with Safaricom and KCB alone making up over 40% of the NSE 20 Share Index. That’s like putting 40% of your portfolio in one stock—and then calling it diversified. Add in the fact that financials and telecoms dominate the index, and you’ve got a recipe for sector-specific shocks. When the shilling weakens, banks take a hit (higher funding costs, NPLs rise). When consumer spending tightens, Safaricom’s M-Pesa revenue slows. The NSE’s liquidity constraints mean you can’t just pivot to small-cap stocks on a whim—those positions are often illiquid, and selling in a panic means taking a haircut.

Take **Safaricom’s 2024 dividend cut**. Investors who had 30% of their portfolio in Safaricom watched their income stream dry up overnight. The stock dropped 12% in a week, and anyone over-exposed felt the pain. Risk management would’ve told them: *Spread that exposure*. Or consider **KCB’s 2023 rights issue**, where investors who had piled into the bank without considering sector limits got diluted when the stock price slumped post-issue. The lesson? Even blue-chips can become traps if you’re not watching your weightings.

# Practical Example

Let’s say you’ve got KES 1,000,000 invested in the NSE. Here’s how a real-world risk check might look:

**Your Portfolio Breakdown:**
- Safaricom: 35% (KES 350,000) @ KES 22.50
- KCB: 25% (KES 250,000) @ KES 45.00
- Equity Bank: 20% (KES 200,000) @ KES 35.00
- Bamburi Cement: 10% (KES 100,000) @ KES 150.00
- Treasury Bonds (5-year): 10% (KES 100,000) @ 12.5% yield

**Step 1: Calculate Your Concentration Risk**
Your top two holdings (Safaricom + KCB) make up 60% of your portfolio. That’s **way above the 10% rule**. If Safaricom drops 10% and KCB drops 8%, your portfolio just lost KES 63,000 (6.3%)—before any other stocks move.

**Step 2: Check Sector Exposure**
- Telecoms (Safaricom): 35%
- Banking (KCB + Equity): 45%
- Cement: 10%
- Fixed Income: 10%

You’re **90% exposed to just two sectors**. If the Central Bank hikes rates again, banks get squeezed. If the shilling weakens, Safaricom’s earnings take a hit. Your cement and bond positions are too small to offset the damage.

**Step 3: Stress Test Your Portfolio**
Let’s simulate a worst-case scenario:
- Safaricom drops 15% (KES 22.50 → KES 19.13)
- KCB drops 12% (KES 45.00 → KES 39.60)
- Equity Bank drops 10% (KES 35.00 → KES 31.50)
- Bamburi Cement drops 5% (KES 150.00 → KES 142.50)
- Bonds remain flat (no change in yield)

**New Portfolio Value:**
- Safaricom: KES 302,955
- KCB: KES 222,000
- Equity Bank: KES 176,250
- Bamburi: KES 95,250
- Bonds: KES 100,000
**Total: KES 896,455**

You just lost **KES 103,545 (10.35%)** in a single quarter. And this assumes only your top holdings moved—what if the entire market sells off?

**The Fix:**
Rebalance to no single stock exceeding 10% of your portfolio. For example:
- Sell KES 150,000 of Safaricom (reduce to 20%)
- Sell KES 100,000 of KCB (reduce to 15%)
- Sell KES 50,000 of Equity Bank (reduce to 15%)
- Add KES 300,000 to smaller-cap stocks (e.g., Carbacid, Crown Paints) or diversify into REITs

**New Breakdown:**
- Safaricom: 20%
- KCB: 15%
- Equity Bank: 15%
- Bamburi: 10%
- Carbacid: 10%
- Crown Paints: 10%
- REITs: 10%
- Bonds: 10%

Now, your worst single-stock hit is 20% (Safaricom), and your sector exposure is spread across 5+ industries. In the same stress test, your loss drops to **KES 65,000 (6.5%)**—a 38% reduction in damage.

# Common Mistakes

**Mistake 1: Chasing Yield Without Checking Weightings**
Investors pile into high-dividend stocks like Safaricom and KCB, thinking they’re "safe" because they pay dividends. But if 40% of your portfolio is in two dividend stocks, you’re not earning income—you’re betting on two companies. The cost? When those stocks drop, your portfolio drops harder than a boda-boda without brakes. In 2023, investors who had 50% in Safaricom and KCB watched their portfolios lose 18% in a year when the broader market was flat.

**Mistake 2: Ignoring Liquidity Risk in Small-Cap Stocks**
You think you’re diversifying by adding a small-cap like Carbacid or Crown Paints, but if you can’t sell it in a panic, it’s not diversification—it’s a trap. The NSE’s small-cap stocks often have daily volumes under KES 5 million. Try selling KES 100,000 worth of shares in a downturn, and you’ll get a 5-10% haircut. The Kenyan market’s liquidity constraints make over-exposure to illiquid stocks a silent killer.

**Mistake 3: Assuming Fixed Income is Always Safe**
Treasury bonds look like a risk-free haven, but in a rising-rate environment, their prices drop. If you’re holding a 5-year bond bought at 12% yield and rates rise to 14%, your bond’s market value just fell 8%. Worse, if you’re forced to sell before maturity, you lock in the loss. Fixed income isn’t risk-free—it’s just *different* risk.

**Mistake 4: Forgetting Currency Risk**
If you’re holding dollar-denominated assets (like some corporate bonds or offshore stocks), a weakening shilling can erase gains. In 2022, the shilling lost 15% against the dollar. Investors who had 20% in dollar assets saw their KES-denominated returns vanish overnight. Always ask: *What happens if the shilling drops 10%?*

# Checklist

- **Verify your top 5 holdings.** If any single stock is over 10% of your portfolio, it’s time to trim. Use your broker’s portfolio tracker or a simple spreadsheet.
- **Check sector exposure.** If banking + telecoms > 60% of your portfolio, you’re playing Russian roulette with sector-specific shocks.
- **Test liquidity.** Can you sell 20% of your portfolio in a day without moving the market? If not, you’re over-exposed to illiquid stocks.
- **Stress-test your portfolio.** Plug in a 10% drop for your top 3 holdings and a 5% shilling devaluation. What’s your worst-case loss? If it’s more than 15%, rebalance.
