Valuation is the process of determining what a company is truly worth, not just what the market says it is worth today. For Kenyan investors trading on the Nairobi Securities Exchange, this means looking beyond share price movements and understanding the underlying business. The goal is to identify companies that generate consistent profits, convert those profits into real cash, and have a clear path to grow those profits over time. A sound valuation framework helps you avoid overpaying for stocks and reduces the risk of permanent capital loss.

At the heart of valuation are four key concepts: earnings, cash flow, growth, and margin of safety. Earnings tell you how much profit a company makes, but not all earnings are equal. Some companies report high profits on paper but struggle to collect cash from customers, while others may reinvest profits to fuel future growth. Cash flow, particularly free cash flow, shows how much money the business actually generates after covering its operating costs and capital expenditures. Growth measures how quickly earnings and cash flow are expanding, which is critical for justifying higher valuations. Finally, margin of safety is the cushion you build by buying a stock at a price significantly below its estimated intrinsic value, protecting you from unexpected downturns or miscalculations.

On the NSE, these concepts play out in real time with companies like Safaricom, Equity Bank, and BAT Kenya. Take Safaricom, for example. Its earnings per share have grown steadily over the past five years, driven by mobile money and data services. However, its free cash flow tells a slightly different story, as the company has had to invest heavily in network expansion and regulatory compliance. Investors who focus solely on earnings might overlook these cash outflows, while those who track free cash flow can better assess the company’s ability to pay dividends or reinvest in growth. Growth is another critical factor; Safaricom’s revenue from M-Pesa and fintech services has outpaced traditional voice and SMS, justifying its premium valuation compared to peers.

A practical example from today’s market is Stanbic Holdings, whose FY2025 earnings were recently flagged for review. Suppose Stanbic reports earnings per share of KES 20, with a current share price of KES 120. This gives it a price-to-earnings ratio of 6, which appears cheap compared to the NSE’s historical average. However, earnings alone don’t tell the full story. If Stanbic’s free cash flow is only KES 10 per share, the stock may not be as attractive, as it suggests the company is struggling to convert profits into cash. Additionally, if earnings growth has stalled due to rising loan defaults or slower economic activity, the low P/E ratio might reflect underlying business challenges rather than a bargain. This is where margin of safety comes in—buying at KES 80 instead of KES 120 provides a buffer if earnings or cash flow disappoint.

Common mistakes investors make when valuing stocks include overemphasizing short-term price movements, ignoring cash flow, and assuming past growth will continue indefinitely. Many retail investors in Kenya fixate on dividend yields without considering whether the company can sustain those payouts. For instance, a high dividend yield might look appealing, but if the company is borrowing to pay dividends or its cash flow is declining, the yield could be unsustainable. Another mistake is relying too heavily on price-to-earnings ratios without adjusting for one-time gains or losses. A company might report a low P/E ratio because it sold an asset, not because its core business is thriving. Finally, investors often underestimate the impact of macroeconomic factors, such as interest rates or currency fluctuations, which can erode earnings and cash flow over time.

To apply these concepts effectively, start by gathering the right data. Review a company’s income statement to understand its earnings, but always cross-check with the cash flow statement to see how much of those earnings translate into real money. Look at the balance sheet to assess debt levels and liquidity, as high debt can strain cash flow and limit growth. For growth, examine revenue and earnings trends over at least five years, paying attention to whether growth is coming from core operations or one-time events. Finally, calculate a margin of safety by comparing your estimated intrinsic value to the current share price. If the stock is trading at a 30% discount to your estimate, you have a reasonable buffer against errors or market volatility.

Before making any investment decision, ask yourself these questions. Does the company generate consistent free cash flow, or is it reliant on debt or asset sales to fund operations? Is earnings growth supported by real business expansion, or is it driven by accounting adjustments or temporary market conditions? What are the risks to the company’s cash flow, such as regulatory changes, competition, or economic downturns? Have you accounted for macroeconomic factors like interest rates, inflation, or currency movements that could impact profitability? And most importantly, are you buying the stock at a price that provides a meaningful margin of safety, or are you paying a premium based on hope rather than fundamentals?

Valuation is both an art and a science, and no framework guarantees success. However, by focusing on earnings, cash flow, growth, and margin of safety, you can make more informed decisions and avoid the pitfalls that trap many retail investors. The NSE offers plenty of opportunities, but they require patience, discipline, and a willingness to dig deeper than the headlines.
