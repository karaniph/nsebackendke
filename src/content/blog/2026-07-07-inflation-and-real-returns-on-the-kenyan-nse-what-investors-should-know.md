Inflation is the rate at which prices for goods and services rise, and it matters for every investor because it erodes purchasing power. A nominal return on an investment is the percentage gain you see in your account, but the real return is what you actually keep after inflation. In simple terms, real return tells you how much you have left to buy goods after prices have risen. For Kenyan investors, inflation interacts with local interest rates, exchange-rate moves, and the earnings climate on the Nairobi Securities Exchange. Understanding real returns helps you compare asset classes on an apples-to-apples basis rather than chasing headline gains.

Today the NSE was quiet, reflecting thin liquidity and a narrow pattern of price action. The NSE20 index stood at 3,844.43 with the market moving modestly higher, while the NASI hovered around the 228 area and turnover was reported around 488.40 million shillings. The central bank’s policy rate sits at 8.75%, and nearby money-market rates were quoted in the mid-to-high single digits, with the 91-day instrument at about 8.835% in the context of the day’s activity. Market commentary highlighted a lack of clear leadership and a thin session, with traders cautious ahead of clearer cues. These conditions matter because inflation expectations shape earnings momentum, discount rates, and how investors price risk on equities.

To understand real returns, it helps to separate what is earned from what prices allow you to buy. Nominal returns on equity come from price appreciation and any dividends paid, but inflation reduces the purchasing power of those dollars or shillings over time. Real returns adjust for inflation, providing a clearer view of wealth growth after price changes. In the Kenyan context, inflation interacts with local monetary policy, fiscal signals, and liquidity conditions, so the real-return picture can differ from simple headline gains. The market’s current muted momentum means that even seemingly solid nominal gains may look modest once inflation is accounted for, especially if inflation surprises to the upside or if discount rates rise on optimism about growth slowing.

A practical link to today’s context is the dividend information that accompanies the market’s broad backdrop. SCOM issued a final dividend of 1.15, and KCB announced a final dividend of 3.00, signaling cash returns that contribute to nominal performance. In a thin-liquidity environment, those cash yields may be more volatile in price terms as traders react to news and shifting risk appetite. Inflation matters here because higher price growth can erode the real value of those dividend streams even when the nominal amount looks attractive. Similarly, bond-market signals, such as the CBK rate and treasury yields, influence the discount rate applied to equities and can compress or extend the real value of future earnings when investors price in inflation expectations.

For fixed-income assets, inflation often presents a direct challenge to real returns. If inflation accelerates while yields on government and corporate debt do not rise enough to compensate, the real payoff from those investments can shrink. The market’s current context, with a policy rate at 8.75% and short-dated yields in the broader market, underscores the sensitivity of real returns to inflation surprises. Equities with pricing power and resilient cash flows tend to fare better in inflationary environments because they can pass some cost increases through to customers, supporting both nominal gains and better real outcomes. The thin liquidity backdrop adds a layer of nuance, as price movements on mega-caps may generate volatile signals that tempt investors to overreact to short-term inflation chatter rather than focusing on long-run real value.

Investors should be mindful of common mistakes when inflation is a central concern. One frequent misstep is evaluating performance solely on nominal gains without adjusting for the eroding effect of inflation on purchasing power. Another is assuming that high dividend yields automatically translate into strong real returns; dividends must be weighed against price changes and inflation. A third pitfall is chasing yield in markets with thin liquidity, where apparent bargains can vanish quickly if supply and demand dynamics shift. Finally, ignoring the impact of inflation on discount rates and earnings expectations can lead to overestimating the true attractiveness of a stock or bond in a given year.

To navigate inflation and real returns effectively, start by comparing nominal returns to your inflation expectations rather than to a bare headline figure. Consider how your portfolio’s mix—equities, fixed income, and cash—performs after adjusting for expected price growth. Pay attention to the quality and pricing power of the companies you own, not just their reported dividend yields, since inflation can influence both margins and earnings growth. Keep a close eye on liquidity conditions and how they shape entry and exit prices in a thin-market environment, especially around mega-caps that can drive broader sentiment. Regularly revisit your target real return and adjust your mix if inflation runs persistently above or below expectations, rather than chasing short-term nominal gains. Diversification across assets with different inflation sensitivities can help smooth real returns over time. Align your risk tolerance with a realistic inflation-adjusted plan and maintain a disciplined approach to rebalancing when markets display volatility tied to inflation news.
