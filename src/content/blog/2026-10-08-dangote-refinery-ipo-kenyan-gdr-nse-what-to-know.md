Kenyan retail investors now have a way into one of Africa's largest share offers without opening a Nigerian brokerage account. The Dangote Petroleum Refinery and Petrochemicals FZE (DPRP) offer has a shilling-priced tranche that closes on Tuesday, 13 October 2026, and the instruments are meant to list on the Nairobi Securities Exchange afterwards.

## The short version

- The Kenyan tranche is a Global Depositary Receipt (GDR) programme, priced at KES 53.50 per GDR. One GDR represents one DPRP share.
- The minimum application is 2,000 GDRs, or KES 107,000. Above that, applications go up in blocks of 100 GDRs.
- Up to roughly 729 million GDRs are on offer, with a target of about KES 39 billion.
- The Capital Markets Authority approved the short form prospectus on 5 October, and the Kenyan window opened on 6 October.
- The underlying offer is 4.1 billion new shares at ₦525 each, listing on the Nigerian Exchange (NGX) main board.

This offer concerns the refinery in Lagos. It is not connected to the proposed Lamu refinery project, and the offer documents say so explicitly.

## What a GDR means for you

A depositary receipt is a certificate that stands in for shares of a foreign company. You pay in shillings, hold the receipt in your CDS account, and eventually trade it on the NSE instead of dealing on the Nigerian market directly.

Some practical consequences:

- Dividends, if any, reach you in shillings, converted from naira or dollars and reduced by fees and withholding tax.
- GDR holders do not vote individually, and they do not qualify for the retail incentive programme attached to the Nigerian offer.
- If demand is higher than the GDR allocation, applications can be scaled back, subject to CMA approval of the allotment basis.

## Dates to keep in your diary

- 13 October: offer closes.
- 27 October: collation of returns completes.
- 11 November: expected allotment.
- Within five business days of allotment: refunds for unsuccessful or surplus applications, paid without interest.
- Early December: expected NSE listing, around 15 business days after allotment.

Your money is tied up for roughly a month before trading begins, so check that the cash is genuinely idle.

## What the numbers say

The financial history is short and dramatic. Revenue climbed from about US$6.3 billion in 2024 to US$12.3 billion in 2025, then reached US$13.9 billion in the first half of 2026 alone. Refinery utilisation moved from about 35% in 2024 to 63% in 2025 and 86% in the first half of this year.

Profit followed the same arc. The company lost roughly US$1.5 billion in 2024 and US$476 million in 2025, then booked a US$1.8 billion profit in the first six months of 2026, on EBITDA of about US$2.6 billion. Net debt fell from US$5.2 billion at the end of 2025 to about US$1.4 billion in June.

At ₦525, Faida Investment Bank's analysts calculate the pre-listing value at around US$46 billion, or about 9.2 times enterprise value to annualised first-half EBITDA and 12.7 times annualised earnings. The word that matters there is annualised. Those multiples double a single strong half-year, and the company itself says that period is not directly comparable with full years.

## The case for the offer

- Africa uses roughly 4.1 million barrels of refined products a day but has only about 1.8 million barrels of refining capacity, so imports fill a large gap.
- The plant was designed for 650,000 barrels a day and has run above that, with throughput of up to 700,000 in June.
- Management plans to roughly double capacity to 1.4 million barrels a day by 2029 with a second refining train.

## Reasons to be careful

- Thin profit record. One profitable half-year follows two loss-making years.
- Margin exposure. Earnings depend on the spread between crude costs and fuel prices, which swings with global markets.
- Single-site risk. The refining, power and logistics assets sit on one coastal site in Lagos.
- Crude supply. About 60% of feedstock in 2025 came from Nigerian grades, so supply policy and security matter.
- Small float and tight control. Public investors would hold about 3.3% of the company, and Aliko Dangote would keep roughly 84% after the offer.
- Governance gaps. Three of ten directors are independent, and the board committees had not met when the prospectus was published.
- Funding gap. The roughly US$1.55 billion raised covers only about 11% of a US$14.3 billion expansion plan. The rest must come from cash flow and debt.
- Tax. Free zone incentives depend on limiting sales into Nigeria's customs territory, and from 2028 profits on those sales may be fully taxed. By volume, the export share dropped from about 62% to 42.5% between early 2025 and early 2026.
- Currency. You pay in shillings for an asset whose cash flows are tied to naira and dollars, and no dividend payout ratio has been set.
- Approvals. The GDR offer needs a minimum success level and Nigerian SEC approval for the GDR listing. Without that approval, the GDRs are not issued or listed, and investors would hold the underlying shares through an omnibus account, with liquidity coming from the NGX.

## Our read for NSE investors

The listing, if it happens, would put a very large foreign name on the NSE. How easily the GDRs trade after listing is unknown, because there is no track record yet, and the underlying float is small. That points to a position sized for a long holding period, with money you will not need while the offer settles. Faida's own view is that the offer suits medium to long-term investors who accept refining-margin and currency swings, and that framing is sensible.
