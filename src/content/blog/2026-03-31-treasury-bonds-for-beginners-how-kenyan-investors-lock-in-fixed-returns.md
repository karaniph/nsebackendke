# Treasury bonds for beginners: How Kenyan investors lock in fixed returns

## Concept

Treasury bonds are debt securities issued by the Kenyan government to fund public spending. They pay a fixed interest rate—called the coupon—every six months until maturity, when the principal is repaid. The price you pay today determines your effective yield, which moves inversely to the bond’s market price. The relationship is captured by the present value formula:

**Price = Σ [Coupon / (1 + Yield)^t] + Principal / (1 + Yield)^n**

Here, *t* is the time to each coupon payment and *n* is the bond’s maturity. When market yields rise, existing bonds with lower coupons lose value because new bonds offer higher returns. Conversely, falling yields push prices up. Duration measures this sensitivity: a bond with a duration of 5 years will lose approximately 5% in value for every 1% rise in yields.

For Kenyan investors, Treasury bonds provide a hedge against equity volatility. Unlike stocks, their cash flows are guaranteed by the sovereign, making them ideal for conservative portfolios. The coupon rate is set at auction, typically above the Central Bank Rate to attract demand. Secondary market trading allows investors to buy or sell before maturity, but prices fluctuate with interest-rate expectations and credit conditions.

## NSE Context

On the Nairobi Securities Exchange, Treasury bonds trade under codes like **KN26** (maturing in 2026) and **KN30** (maturing in 2030). These instruments dominate the fixed-income segment, accounting for over 70% of daily bond turnover. For example, the KN26 bond, issued in 2021 at a 10.5% coupon, now trades at a slight premium due to falling benchmark rates. Its duration of 2.3 years makes it less sensitive to rate hikes than longer-dated bonds like KN35 (duration 8.1 years), which has seen sharper price swings amid inflation concerns.

Recent NSE retail investors increasingly prefer Treasury bonds over bank deposits, drawn by yields above 12% for 10-year paper. However, liquidity remains uneven: shorter-duration bonds like KN26 trade more frequently, while KN35 often requires patience to exit. Corporate issuers like Safaricom and KCB Group have also tapped the market with bonds yielding 13–15%, but these carry issuer-specific risks absent in Treasury paper. The NSE’s automated trading system ensures transparent pricing, but bid-ask spreads widen during volatile sessions, particularly ahead of Treasury auctions.

## Practical Example

Consider the **KN30 bond**, issued in June 2023 with a 12.5% annual coupon and maturing in June 2030. An investor buys KES 1 million face value at a market price of 102.50 (KES 1,025,000) when the yield to maturity is 11.8%. Here’s how the cash flows break down:

1. **Annual Coupon Payments**: 12.5% of KES 1 million = KES 125,000 every year, paid semi-annually as KES 62,500.
2. **Principal Repayment**: KES 1 million at maturity in June 2030.
3. **Yield Calculation**: The bond’s yield to maturity (11.8%) reflects the total return if held to maturity, accounting for the premium paid (KES 25,000 over face value).

If the investor sells the bond in March 2026 after holding it for 2.75 years, the price will depend on prevailing yields. Suppose 10-year Treasury yields have fallen to 10.5% due to a dovish Central Bank policy. The bond’s price would rise to approximately 105.20 (KES 1,052,000), generating a capital gain of KES 27,000. Combined with coupon income of KES 156,250 (2.5 years of payments), the total return is KES 183,250, or 18.3% over 2.75 years. This illustrates why longer-duration bonds reward patience when rates decline.

The reverse holds if yields rise. A 1% increase in yields would push the bond’s price down to ~97.80 (KES 978,000), eroding part of the coupon gains. This sensitivity underscores the importance of matching bond duration to investment horizon.

## Common Mistakes

- **Chasing high coupons without checking duration.** Investors often fixate on bonds offering 14% yields, only to discover they’re 15-year paper with a duration of 11 years. In a rising-rate environment, such bonds can lose 10% in value for every 1% rise in yields. For example, the KN40 bond (2040 maturity) fell 8% in Q1 2025 when the Treasury signaled tighter monetary policy. Shorter-duration bonds like KN28 (2028 maturity) are less exposed.

- **Ignoring reinvestment risk.** A 5-year bond with a 12% coupon locks in that rate for half a decade, but if rates fall to 9% in two years, reinvesting future coupons at lower yields reduces total returns. This is particularly acute for investors relying on bond income, such as retirees. The KN26 bond’s 10.5% coupon will face reinvestment pressure if the Central Bank cuts rates aggressively.

- **Overpaying for liquidity.** Retail investors often buy bonds at inflated prices during auctions, only to sell at a loss when liquidity dries up. The KN30 bond, for instance, traded at 104.00 in the secondary market in February 2026, but bid prices dropped to 101.50 in March as sellers outnumbered buyers. Always check the NSE’s daily turnover data before committing capital.

## Checklist

- **Match duration to your horizon.** Use bonds with maturities within your investment timeframe. For a 3-year goal, KN26 or KN27 are suitable; for 10 years, KN35 or KN40 may fit. Avoid mismatches that force early sales at unfavorable prices.

- **Compare yields to bank deposits.** Treasury bonds must outperform fixed deposits by at least 200 basis points to justify the liquidity trade-off. As of March 2026, the best 12-month deposit offers 11.5%, while 5-year Treasury bonds yield 12.8%. The spread is marginal, so consider tax implications: bond coupons are taxed at 15%, while deposit interest is taxed at your marginal rate.

- **Monitor auction schedules.** Treasury issues new bonds quarterly. The KN30 bond’s next auction is scheduled for April 2026; participating early can secure a higher coupon if rates are rising. Set calendar reminders to review auction results published by the Central Bank.

- **Use the NSE bond calculator.** The exchange provides a tool to estimate yields based on price and maturity. Input your target purchase price to see if the yield meets your required return. For example, a KN30 priced at 103.00 yields 11.5%, below the 12.5% coupon rate due to the premium paid.
