# Concept

Treasury bills (T-bills) are the bedrock of Kenya’s fixed-income market, serving as the primary risk-free asset for both institutional and retail investors. Issued by the Kenyan government with tenors of 91, 182, and 364 days, these zero-coupon securities trade at a discount to face value, with the difference constituting the investor’s return. The yield is derived from the formula:

**Yield = [(Face Value - Purchase Price) / Purchase Price] × (365 / Tenor in Days)**

This discount mechanism ensures predictable returns at maturity, making T-bills a cornerstone of conservative portfolios. The structure aligns with Kenya’s monetary policy framework, where the Central Bank of Kenya (CBK) sets rates to manage liquidity and inflation expectations. Retail investors can participate directly through primary auctions or the secondary market, with minimum denominations of KES 100,000 for primary purchases and KES 3,000 via the NSE’s M-Akiba platform.

The economic rationale for T-bills is rooted in their risk-free status in nominal terms, as the government guarantees repayment. Their yields serve as the benchmark for Kenya’s risk-free rate, adjusted for liquidity and term premiums. For Kenyan investors, T-bills provide a reference point for pricing other fixed-income instruments, including corporate bonds and bank deposits. The absence of credit risk makes them ideal for capital preservation, while their liquidity supports portfolio rebalancing and tactical asset allocation.

T-bill yields are highly sensitive to monetary policy shifts. When the CBK tightens policy to combat inflation, T-bill yields rise, reflecting higher borrowing costs for the government. Conversely, dovish policy shifts compress yields, reducing returns for new investors. The interplay between T-bill yields and inflation expectations is particularly pronounced in Kenya, where food and fuel price volatility often drives policy responses. For example, during periods of elevated inflation, the CBK has historically auctioned T-bills at yields exceeding 15%, as seen in the March 2026 auction where the 91-day bill priced at 14.5%.

The term structure of T-bill yields also provides insights into market expectations. A steepening yield curve—where longer tenors offer significantly higher yields—suggests expectations of rising interest rates or inflation. Conversely, a flat or inverted curve may signal expectations of monetary easing. Kenyan investors closely monitor this dynamic, as it influences decisions on rolling over maturing T-bills or extending duration for higher returns.

On the Nairobi Securities Exchange (NSE), T-bills are the most liquid government securities, with weekly turnover often exceeding KES 5 billion. The 91-day tenor dominates retail investor participation due to its frequent issuance and alignment with short-term cash flow needs. For instance, during the March 2026 auction, the 91-day bill priced at a 14.5% annualized yield, reflecting tight liquidity conditions in the banking sector. The 182-day and 364-day tenors, while less liquid, attract institutional investors seeking to match liabilities or hedge longer-term obligations.

Corporate issuers frequently reference T-bill yields when pricing commercial paper and term deposits. In Q1 2026, Safaricom’s KES 10 billion commercial paper series priced at a spread of 120 basis points over the 182-day T-bill, underscoring the instrument’s role as a pricing anchor for corporate debt. Similarly, banks such as Equity Group and Co-operative Bank adjust deposit rates in response to T-bill yields, creating a feedback loop between government borrowing costs and private sector funding. This relationship is particularly evident during periods of tight liquidity, when banks compete aggressively for retail deposits by offering rates closely tied to T-bill yields.

The secondary market for T-bills is fragmented but growing, with institutional investors like ICEA Lion and Sanlam Kenya dominating turnover. Retail participation remains constrained by minimum investment thresholds and limited awareness, though the NSE’s M-Akiba platform has democratized access by allowing purchases in denominations as low as KES 3,000. This shift has broadened the investor base, particularly among younger Kenyans seeking alternatives to fixed deposits. The platform’s integration with mobile money has further reduced barriers to entry, enabling seamless participation in primary auctions.

The NSE’s role in T-bill trading extends beyond liquidity provision. The exchange facilitates price discovery through its secondary market, where bid-ask spreads tighten during periods of high demand. For example, the 91-day T-bill’s bid-ask spread narrowed to 5 basis points in February 2026, reflecting robust investor appetite. This liquidity is critical for institutional investors managing large portfolios, as it allows for efficient execution of trades without significant price impact.

The interplay between T-bill yields and equity market performance is another key consideration for NSE investors. When T-bill yields rise, equities often face headwinds as investors rotate into risk-free assets. Conversely, compressed T-bill yields can encourage risk-taking in equities, particularly in sectors sensitive to interest rates such as banking and real estate. This dynamic was evident in early 2026, when a rally in T-bill yields coincided with a pullback in the NSE 20 Share Index, as investors reassessed risk premiums.

# Practical Example

Consider a retail investor purchasing a 182-day T-bill with a face value of KES 100,000 at a discount price of KES 93,000. The yield calculation proceeds as follows:

1. **Discount Return**: KES 100,000 - KES 93,000 = KES 7,000
2. **Annualized Yield**: (7,000 / 93,000) × (365 / 182) = 15.3%

This yield compares favorably to the average 12.8% offered by Kenyan banks on 6-month fixed deposits. The investor’s effective return is locked in at maturity, with no reinvestment risk unless they roll over into a new T-bill. The calculation assumes no early sale, as secondary market pricing fluctuates with interest rate expectations.

For context, the March 2026 auction saw the 182-day bill priced at a 15.1% yield, slightly below the example’s hypothetical rate. This reflects the CBK’s tightening bias, aimed at curbing inflationary pressures from rising fuel and food costs. The auction’s stop-out yield—where demand exceeds supply—was set at 15.1%, indicating strong investor demand for government paper.

The investor’s net return is subject to capital gains tax at 15%, reducing the effective yield to 12.96%. This tax treatment is consistent across all T-bill tenors, making it a critical factor in comparing returns with other fixed-income instruments. For high-net-worth investors, the tax impact is more pronounced, as the 15% rate applies to the full discount, not just the income component.

Secondary market dynamics further illustrate the importance of timing. If the investor sells the T-bill before maturity in a rising rate environment, the price will decline to reflect the higher yield demanded by new investors. For example, in February 2026, secondary market prices for the 182-day T-bill fell by 0.8% when the CBK signaled a more hawkish stance. This underscores the need for investors to align T-bill holdings with their liquidity needs and risk tolerance.

# Common Mistakes

- **Ignoring the discount pricing mechanics**: Some investors mistakenly treat T-bills as coupon-paying bonds, leading to miscalculations of expected returns. The yield is derived solely from the discount, not periodic income. This error often results in overestimating returns, particularly for those accustomed to bond markets where coupons provide regular cash flows.

- **Overlooking reinvestment risk**: Rolling over maturing T-bills at lower yields can erode returns. In 2025, the 91-day bill yield fell from 15.8% to 13.2%, reducing rollover gains for investors who failed to ladder maturities. This risk is particularly acute during periods of monetary easing, when yields decline sharply.

- **Misjudging liquidity needs**: Selling T-bills before maturity in a rising rate environment can result in capital losses. Secondary market prices fell by 0.8% in February 2026 when the CBK signaled a more hawkish stance. Investors must assess their cash flow requirements and avoid premature sales unless absolutely necessary.

- **Tax inefficiency**: Failing to account for the 15% capital gains tax reduces the net yield by 225 basis points on a 15% gross return. This is particularly costly for high-net-worth investors in higher tax brackets, as the tax applies to the full discount, not just the income component. Strategic tax planning, such as holding T-bills in tax-exempt accounts, can mitigate this impact.

- **Overconcentration in a single tenor**: Investors often allocate disproportionately to the 91-day tenor due to its liquidity and frequent issuance. However, this approach exposes them to reinvestment risk if yields decline. A laddered approach—spreading investments across multiple tenors—can smooth returns and reduce the impact of rate volatility.

- **Neglecting auction timing**: Participating in auctions without considering the CBK’s policy stance can lead to suboptimal yields. For example, bidding aggressively during a tightening cycle may result in lower allocations or higher yields than necessary. Investors should align auction participation with their view on monetary policy and liquidity conditions.

- Monitor monetary policy developments, including inflation data and CBK statements, to anticipate yield movements. Policy shifts can significantly impact T-bill pricing and returns.
