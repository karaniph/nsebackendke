## Why this matters
For a Kenyan retail investor, the allocation between bonds and equities is the primary lever to control risk while pursuing growth. A well-tuned blend protects capital in downturns and still leaves room for upside when equities rally. In a market that today is described as thin with liquidity risk and uncertain foreign flows, knowing how to tilt toward bonds or toward stocks based on your horizon and risk tolerance becomes even more valuable.

## The concept
Bonds are fixed income instruments that promise regular coupons and return of principal at maturity. Equities are ownership in companies and offer upside from capital appreciation and dividends but come with higher volatility. Allocation is the percentage of your portfolio held in each asset class. The key tradeoffs are risk (how much your value can swing), yield (current income versus growth), and time horizon (how long you plan to invest before needing the money).

Diversification matters because bonds tend to dampen the wobble of equities. When your horizon is long, you can usually tolerate more equity bite for higher long-run returns. When the horizon is short, you tilt toward bonds to preserve capital and maintain liquidity. The practical rule is to align your split with how much risk you can stomach and when you expect to need the cash.

## How it works on the NSE
On the Nairobi Securities Exchange you access two broad streams of risk: stocks and government/sovereign bonds. Equities are traded in shares of listed companies and are exposed to earnings, sentiment, and liquidity cycles. Fixed income comes from Treasury bonds and other money-market instruments, where prices move with interest-rate signals from the Central Bank.

Today’s signals, in context, include a CBK policy rate of 8.75% and a thin-session backdrop. NASI sits around an index level described in reports, NSE 20 trading around the 4,160 area, and turnover hovering at liquidity-dependent levels. In such a setting, bonds can provide steadier carry and a cushion if foreign participation remains uncertain. If new bond issues come to market and yields shift, that will shape the relative attractiveness of short versus long duration. Watch top equity anchors like SCOM, KCB, and EQTY for intraday cues, but keep your eye on the bigger horizon: your allocation posture.

If you’re building a tactical stance, the NSE structure supports rebalancing across these assets as liquidity allows. Equities demand attention to liquidity and price discovery, while bonds respond to interest-rate expectations and auction outcomes. The practical takeaway: your allocation should reflect both current market signals and your long-run plan, not chase daily moves.

## Worked example
Illustrative scenario only. Consider a 1,000,000 KES portfolio designed for a medium-term horizon (5–7 years). The investor has moderate risk tolerance and wants a disciplined approach that can weather a volatile equity period.

Step 1 – Define the baseline split. Start with 60% in equities and 40% in bonds. This is a traditional balance for someone seeking growth with a cushion from fixed income.

Step 2 – Allocate within equities. Divide the equity portion across a few liquid, well-known names and index exposure. For example, place 200,000 KES in SCOM, 200,000 KES in KCB, and 200,000 KES in EQTY, with the remaining 200,000 KES spread across additional large-cap or diversified counters that maintain liquidity.

Step 3 – Allocate within bonds. Invest 240,000 KES in short- to medium-duration Treasury bonds and 160,000 KES in longer-dated issues. The idea is to capture the steady carry from shorter maturities while retaining some exposure to longer-duration yield changes.

Step 4 – Consider a rebalancing cadence. Rebalance annually or whenever the equity portion deviates by more than 5–10 percentage points from the target split. In a thin market, err on the side of smaller, more frequent adjustments rather than big, infrequent shuffles.

Step 5 – Run a simple scenario. If equities return 12% over a year and bonds return 5%, the blended return on a 60/40 split would be roughly 0.6 × 12% + 0.4 × 5% = 7.2% + 2% = 9.2% for the period. If stocks fall by 20% but bonds stay flat, the portfolio would decline by about 0.6 × 20% = 12%. The fixed-income slice softens the blow but does not erase risk, underscoring why horizon and tolerance matter.

Step 6 – Translate to real-market awareness. In practice, you will see market signals from the NSE to guide exact moves. If CBK continues to influence yields and a new bond auction changes the yield curve, you may adjust the bond sleeve or take a light equity tilt depending on liquidity and future rate expectations. The key is to stay disciplined and avoid knee-jerk shifts on every rate rumor.

Step 7 – Document the plan. Write down your target allocation, your minimum acceptable exposure to each class, and your rebalancing rule. Make the plan part of your personal investing routine rather than a one-off decision.

Note: The numbers above are illustrative and meant to demonstrate the mechanics of a 60/40 split. Your actual allocation should reflect your personal risk tolerance, liquidity needs, and time horizon, and you should verify current yields and liquidity before committing capital.

## Common mistakes
- Too much tilt toward equities for short horizons. Risk a material drawdown just when you need cash or want to meet a spending schedule.
- Ignoring liquidity in a thin market. If you cannot exit positions easily, you lose the ability to rebalance or respond to shocks.
- Chasing last period’s winners. Fixed income and equities can diverge; sticking to a plan beats market-timing attempts.
- Overlooking duration risk in bonds. A long-duration bond can swing with rate changes more than you expect; mix maturities to manage this.
- Forgetting to rebalance. Markets drift; without rebalancing, your portfolio can drift far from the target risk profile.

## Your checklist
- Define your investment horizon and risk tolerance in writing.
- Pick a baseline allocation that matches that profile (for many, 60/40 is a starting point).
- Diversify within each class: equities across sectors or counters with liquidity; bonds across maturities.
- Establish a rebalancing rule and stick to it.
- Monitor liquidity and market signals, but avoid knee-jerk shifts based on one data point.
- Verify current yields, auction activity, and policy signals before adjusting weights.

## FAQ
- Is there a right split for everyone
  No. The right split depends on your time horizon, cash needs, and appetite for risk. A longer horizon generally allows more equity exposure, while a shorter horizon favors bonds.
- How often should I rebalance
  Rebalance at least annually, or when a drift exceeds a defined threshold (for example, 5–10 percentage points). In thin or volatile markets, more frequent, smaller adjustments can be prudent.
- Should I chase dividend stocks or growth stocks
  Both can play a role. Dividends provide cash flow and yield, while growth stocks offer upside. Your decision should fit your income needs and risk tolerance, not a single market message.
