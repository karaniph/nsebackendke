## Why this matters
Longer-duration bonds move more when rates change, and that matters for Kenyan retail investors who build bond portfolios in search of steady income. If you’re holding or eyeing long government or corporate issues, a small rate move can translate into a bigger price swing than you expect. The current Kenya context shows a 8.75% central bank rate snapshot and a quiet debt market, so being mindful of duration helps you avoid big surprises in thin sessions.

## The concept
Duration is a measure of a bond’s price sensitivity to small changes in yield. The higher the duration, the bigger the price moves when yields shift. There are different flavors, but the practical takeaway is simple: a bond with a long duration will fall more when yields rise and rise more when yields fall, compared with a bond of shorter duration with the same credit quality and coupon structure. In other words, duration translates rate risk into price risk. The effect compounds over time, especially if you hold to maturity or if you’re rolling over cash at uncertain rates.

## How it works on the NSE
On the Nairobi Securities Exchange, most appetite for fixed income comes from government bonds and some corporates. When the market expects a policy move by the Central Bank or a shift in auction supply, yields move and longer-dated issues tend to show bigger price responses. In thin sessions, liquidity risk amplifies those moves: a small bid or offer can move prices more quickly than in a heavy-trading day. The current snapshot notes a rate environment around 8.75% and ongoing attention to AIB bond issue filings, which can influence yield direction. Traders and investors who focus on duration will tend to see larger price changes in longer maturities than in short ones for the same yield move.

## Worked example
Let us walk through a clear, illustrative scenario to show the magnitude difference between long and short duration bonds.
- Setup: two Kenyan bonds with different durations. Bond A is long-dated with illustrative duration 9 years and Bond B is short-dated with illustrative duration 2 years. Base price for both bonds is 100. Coupon structure is not the central focus here; we just need duration to show sensitivity.
- Yield move: suppose yields rise by 0.50 percentage points (illustrative, not a claim about actual market moves).
- Price impact rule: approximate price change is pricechange ≈ -duration × yieldchange, using duration in years and yieldchange in percentage points.
- Long bond result: pricechange ≈ -9 × 0.50% = -4.5%. If the base price is 100, the new price ≈ 95.5.
- Short bond result: pricechange ≈ -2 × 0.50% = -1.0%. If the base price is 100, the new price ≈ 99.0.
- Takeaway: with the same 0.50 percentage-point move in rates, the longer-duration bond falls about four times as much as the short-duration bond. In the opposite scenario, a rate drop would push the long bond up more than the short one.
- Practical note: in real markets, duration is not the only factor. Convexity, coupon schedule, and liquidity also matter. Still, duration gives you a first-principles sense of the direction and scale of impact.

## Common mistakes
- Confusing maturity with duration. A bond can be long in years but have a low duration if it carries a high coupon.
- Ignoring convexity. For larger rate moves, convexity can tame or amplify the price change beyond the linear duration estimate.
- Underestimating liquidity risk in thin sessions. Low volume can exaggerate price swings on longer bonds.
- Failing to stress-test portfolios. Relying on a single rate scenario misses how diversified timing and coupons affect overall risk.

## Your checklist
- Find the duration for each bond in your watchlist and note which are long vs short.
- Test a hypothetical rate move (for example, 25 or 50 basis points) and compare projected price moves across bonds with different durations.
- Check current liquidity and market depth before chasing long-duration prints on a thin session.
- Use a mix of maturities to balance yield pickup and rate risk; consider laddering or a duration target for your portfolio.

## FAQ
- Q: What exactly is duration, in simple terms?
  A: It’s a measure of how much a bond’s price will move for a given change in yields; higher duration means bigger price swings.
- Q: Should I avoid long-duration bonds when rates look likely to rise?
  A: Not necessarily. If you need income and can tolerate price swings, shorter-duration approaches or laddered exposure can reduce risk while preserving some upside when rates fall.
- Q: How can I apply this on the NSE today?
  A: Start by identifying bonds with clearly different durations, estimate how a plausible rate move would affect them, and observe liquidity and spread conditions during thin sessions.
