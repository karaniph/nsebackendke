## The concept
Reinvestment risk is the chance that cash you receive today—dividends, coupons, or principal from a maturing bond—has to be reinvested at a rate that may be higher or lower than your current yield. For dividend investors, the risk is that future payouts are funded from reinvested cash that earns less than the original payout. For bond investors, coupon reinvestment risk matters because you usually reinvest each coupon into new bonds or money-market instruments at the going rate, which can swing with policy moves and auctions.

Key ideas in plain terms:
- yield: the income return on your investment, expressed as a percentage per year
- coupon: the periodic interest payment a bond delivers
- reinvestment rate: the rate you lock in when you put cash flows back to work

## Worked example
Step-by-step, without pretending exact market numbers:
- Step 1: You receive a cash flow from dividends or a bond coupon.
- Step 2: You reinvest that cash flow into a similar quality instrument at the prevailing reinvestment rate.
- Step 3: Over multiple periods, your total future income depends on how that reinvestment rate compares with the rate your original investment was producing.
- Step 4: If the reinvestment rate is lower than the original yield, your overall cashflow growth slows; if it’s higher, you boost total income; if it’s the same, you preserve the existing trajectory.

Practical takeaway: the actual income you can rely on in future years hinges on the rate environment you lock in when you reinvest funds. If yields drift lower after you receive cash, your ability to maintain the same cashflow level becomes harder unless you diversify or tilt toward instruments with more flexible income profiles. In markets like Kenya, where policy moves and auctions can shift quickly, a plan that accounts for different reinvestment-rate scenarios is worth having.

## Common mistakes
- Assuming reinvestment rate equals the initial yield forever.
- Ignoring the timing of cash flows and liquidity needs.
- Reinvesting all cash into a single name or instrument, increasing concentration risk.
- Overlooking taxes and fees that erode reinvested income.
- Not accounting for inflation and real purchasing power when planning long-term income.
- Failing to use a ladder or glide-path approach to maturity/risk levels.

## Your checklist
- Map your income needs and keep a cash reserve for essentials.
- Run scenarios for rising, flat, and falling reinvestment rates based on current signals (rates, auctions, and central-bank guidance).
- Diversify reinvestment across equities, bonds, and money-market instruments to reduce single-source risk.
- Consider dividend-focused funds or instruments with more predictable reinvestment options where appropriate.
- Review costs, taxes, and liquidity implications before choosing reinvestment paths.
- Revisit the plan after major rate moves or quarterly, whichever comes first.

## FAQ
- Q: Why does reinvestment risk matter more when rates move? A: If rates rise after you receive cash, you may capture higher future income by reinvesting; if they fall, your income growth slows.
- Q: Should I reinvest every dividend? A: Not necessarily. Match reinvestment to your income needs, liquidity, and tax situation; keep enough cash on hand for living expenses.
- Q: How can I mitigate this risk now? A: Build a diversified ladder across maturities and instrument types, maintain cash for needs, and consider products that smooth reinvestment yields.
