## The concept
A yield curve plots the yields of similar-quality government securities across different maturities. The normal (upward-sloping) curve indicates that longer maturities offer higher yields to compensate for the additional time and risk involved. A flat curve signals little difference between short and long yields, while an inverted curve suggests that longer-term yields are lower than short-term ones, which is often interpreted as a sign of stressed growth expectations. In Kenya, the shape of the curve is influenced by multiple forces: Central Bank policy actions, expectations for inflation, and the ongoing demand for longer-dated government bonds. Taken together, these factors shape the relative pricing of short versus long maturities and guide market participants in their assessments of risk, return, and timing.

- For a buyer of long-dated bonds, a steeper curve can offer higher carry, but watch for price sensitivity if rates rally. For a short/bullet strategy, the short end offers liquidity and stability with less duration risk.
Practical takeaway: use the slope to gauge your expected rate path and align your duration with your horizon. If you believe rates will drift higher in coming months, keeping a shorter duration may help avoid price pullbacks; if you expect rate stability or later declines, longer duration could improve total return.

## Common mistakes
- Assuming the curve predicts exact policy moves on a calendar date.
- Mixing short-dated T-bill yields with long-dated bond yields when pricing risk.
- Ignoring liquidity and tax/fees in fixed income that can distort apparent curve signals.
- Over-interpreting small one-day moves as trend changes.
- Using the curve in a vacuum without considering inflation expectations and currency risk.
These missteps can obscure the practical use of the curve as a horizon tool and may lead to unintended exposure if the landscape shifts.

## FAQ
- Q: What does a steepening curve imply for my investments?
  A: It suggests long-term yields are rising faster than short-term yields, which can increase income from longer bonds but also increase price risk if rates keep climbing.
- Q: Can the Kenyan yield curve invert like in some mature markets?
  A: It can, in theory, but it usually signals meaningful shifts in growth and policy expectations; you’d see short-term yields rise relative to long-term yields.
- Q: How often should I check the curve?
  A: Use the curve as a horizon tool rather than a daily trade signal; review when major macro data or policy guidance is released and align with your investment plan.
