## Why this matters
For Kenyan retail investors, the price/earnings ratio, or P/E, is a quick checkpoint to gauge whether a stock looks expensive or cheap relative to its earnings power. It helps you compare apples to apples across price levels, but it’s not a standalone buy signal. Used well, P/E nudges you toward sensible questions about growth, earnings quality, and risk.

## The concept
P/E, short for price-to-earnings ratio, is simple in formula but tricky in interpretation. It measures how much investors are willing to pay for a company’s each shilling of earnings. In plain terms:
- P/E = price per share / earnings per share (EPS).
- EPS is the portion of a company’s profit allocated to each outstanding share, usually over the trailing 12 months (TTM).
- There are two common versions: trailing P/E (based on past earnings) and forward or estimated P/E (based on expected future earnings).

A high P/E can reflect optimism about future growth; a low P/E can signal cheapness or concerns about sustainability. The catch: P/E doesn’t account for debt, cash flow quality, capital needs, or one-off items that can distort earnings. A high P/E on a cyclical business might vanish when the cycle turns, for example. Always place P/E in the context of earnings quality and growth prospects.

## How it works on the NSE
The Nairobi Securities Exchange uses the usual price-per-share data plus earnings numbers reported by listed companies to compute P/E. Here’s how to think about it in the Kenyan market context:
- Price input: use the latest closing price per share, or the price you’d be paying to buy at the market. Liquidity matters; in thinly traded names, the price can move with small trades, skewing day-to-day P/E readings.
- Earnings input: use trailing 12 months earnings per share (TTM EPS) where available. If a company reports irregular earnings or one-off items, the trailing figure can be distorted. Forward P/E uses next year’s expected earnings, which depends on management guidance and analyst estimates.
- Sector context: P/E levels differ by sector due to growth, risk, and asset intensity. Compare a consumer staple with a growth-leaning tech-like business to avoid drawing wrong conclusions from a single number.
- Negative or zero earnings: if EPS is negative, P/E is not meaningful or can be undefined. In such cases, investors focus on alternatives like price-to-sales, debt levels, or cash flow metrics.

In practice, use P/E as one lens among several, and prefer it when you can benchmark against peers and the market’s growth expectations. The NSE’s environment—liquidity, earnings season timing, and local macro shocks—all color how far a single P/E reading should carry you.

## Worked example
- Step 1: Gather inputs. Identify the current price per share (P) and the trailing 12 months earnings per share (E).
- Step 2: Compute the P/E. Calculate the ratio as P divided by E.
- Step 3: Interpret the result. Compare the P/E to the sector median and to peers with similar risk profiles. A higher P/E may reflect expected growth, while a lower one may reflect slower growth or greater risk.
- Step 4: Check the growth signal. Compare the P/E to the company’s growth outlook (the growth rate g). If growth is strong and sustainable, a higher P/E can be justified; if growth looks fragile, the high P/E could be a trap.
- Step 5: Consider forward P/E. If next-year earnings are expected to rise, forward P/E may be lower than trailing P/E, signaling potential upside not captured by the latest earnings release.
- Step 6: Assess earnings quality. Look for recurring profits, avoided one-offs, and stable margins. A high P/E on volatile or one-off earnings is riskier than a high P/E on stable, recurring earnings.

Two clearly illustrative scenarios (no numbers needed):
- Scenario A — price rises while earnings stay flat. P/E climbs. The stock may look expensive versus history, but if the price rise reflects a plausible future growth path supported by solid catalysts, the higher P/E could be justified. Confirm with forward earnings and the durability of the catalysts.
- Scenario B — earnings rise but the price hardly moves. P/E falls. This can signal value emerging, especially if the earnings uptick is durable and margins hold. Verify that the earnings uplift isn’t a one-off and that balance-sheet strength supports ongoing profitability.

Bottom line: use P/E to frame the story, not to decide in a vacuum. Always cross-check with growth prospects, cash flow, and peer comparisons before acting.

## Common mistakes
- - Reading P/E in isolation and ignoring earnings quality.
- - Using trailing P/E when earnings are highly cyclical or distorted by one-offs.
- - Comparing P/E across unrelated sectors with different growth profiles.
- - Failing to consider forward P/E or PEG (price/earnings to growth) when growth is material.
- - Ignoring debt, cash on hand, and capital expenditure needs that affect sustainable earnings.
- - Relying on a single data point during earnings season; wait for the full report and guidance.

## Your checklist
- - Confirm whether the P/E is trailing or forward, and note the earnings basis (TTM vs next year).
- - Compare P/E to peers in the same sector and to the sector median; check for recent earnings revisions.
- - Review earnings quality: recurring profits, margins, and any one-off items.
- - Consider growth prospects and whether the company can sustain earnings growth.
- - Look at other metrics alongside P/E: dividend yield, ROE, debt levels, and cash flow.

## FAQ
- Q: Why can a high P/E be justified? A: If the company has strong, durable growth prospects and healthy earnings quality, investors may pay a premium for future earnings power.
- Q: When is P/E not meaningful? A: When earnings are negative, highly volatile, or heavily skewed by one-offs; in those cases, P/E can mislead.
- Q: Should I rely only on P/E? A: No. Use P/E with forward estimates, growth expectations, and complementary metrics for a fuller picture.
